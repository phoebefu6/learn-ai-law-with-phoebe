The AI-ready legal team & legal ops: people first, then tools

The bottleneck in legal AI is not the technology - it is that only about a third of lawyers feel ready to use it. This session is about closing that gap: the roles that change, the skills worth teaching (verification above all), why training beats mandates, and how to choose legal tech without buying a hallucination problem. You walk out with a team skills-map and a vendor-vetting checklist you can use on the next tool that lands on your desk.

Part 0

Why readiness is a people problem before a tool problem

Roughly 80% of firms are investing in AI, but only about 35% of lawyers feel their teams have adequate AI skills. That gap is the whole story. You can license the best legal-AI platform on the market and still get poor, risky work if your lawyers do not know how to prompt it, when to distrust it, and how to verify what it produces. This session treats AI readiness as a capability you build in people, supported by tools you vet carefully - not the other way round. The ethics floor from session a3 is assumed throughout; here we build the team that can stand on it.

Part 1 · covers the readiness gap + the roles that change

Closing the skills gap 8 min live

Readiness is not one skill spread evenly - it is a small number of roles that change, sitting on a base of shared literacy every lawyer needs. Think of it as a ladder: shared AI literacy at the bottom, then the AI-literate lawyer, then a legal-ops or AI champion, then leaders who set the culture. You are building the whole ladder, not just the top.

LiveThe roles that change - and the one everyone shares4 min▶

AI does not create dozens of new job titles. It reshapes a few roles and adds one shared baseline.

  • The AI-literate lawyer. Not a specialist - every practising lawyer. They can prompt a tool usefully, they verify its output as a reflex, and they know the situations where AI is more liability than help. This is the role most of your training budget should build.
  • The legal-ops or AI champion. One person, or a small group, who vets tools, designs the workflows, and spreads what works across the team. Without a named champion, AI adoption fragments into private habits nobody can see or supervise.
  • Shared literacy underneath. Below both, every lawyer needs the same base: what AI is, where it fails, and the confidentiality line they must never cross. This is the foundation the ethics session set.
Questions to ask your team Who is our named AI or legal-ops champion - and if nobody, who should it be? Which of our lawyers are genuinely AI-literate versus quietly avoiding it? Does every lawyer share the same baseline understanding of AI's limits and the confidentiality line?
LiveWhy training beats mandates4 min▶

The instinct under adoption pressure is to mandate use - "everyone will use the tool by Q3". It is the wrong lever, and often a dangerous one.

  • A mandate without skill manufactures risk. Requiring lawyers to use AI before they can verify its output is how fabricated citations reach filings. You get usage metrics and a liability at the same time.
  • Training builds the judgment a mandate assumes. The skill that matters - knowing when to distrust the tool - only comes from practice with feedback, not from a directive.
  • Culture carries it further than policy. When your best lawyers visibly verify and own AI output, that norm spreads. When leaders fund training and model the behaviour, readiness compounds; when they only mandate tools, it stalls.
Real world

The metric that misled. A legal team hit 90% AI-tool adoption in a quarter and called it a win - until a near-miss on an unverified citation showed that adoption had outrun skill. They kept the tool, slowed the rollout, and put verification training first. Usage dipped, then recovered on a firmer footing. Readiness, not adoption, was the number that mattered.

Questions to ask your team Are we measuring adoption or readiness? What would our lawyers do with an AI answer they suspect is wrong - and have we taught that, or just assumed it?
Part 2 · covers the skills to build + reskill vs hire

The skills, and a verification culture 7 min live

Four skills carry most of the value, and they are not equal. Verification discipline is the one that protects the license; the others make the tool useful. The good news for a leader: nearly all of this is reskilling existing lawyers, not hiring new ones.

LiveThe four skills - and the one that outranks the rest4 min▶

Rank them honestly, because your training will follow the ranking.

  • Verification discipline (the #1 skill). The habit of treating every AI output as a draft to confirm against primary law before it is relied on or filed. This is the skill that keeps a lawyer out of the sanctions story - teach it first and hardest.
  • Prompt literacy. Asking a tool for what you actually need, with the right context, and recognising a weak answer. Useful, learnable quickly, but worthless without verification behind it.
  • Confidentiality awareness. Knowing instinctively what may and may not go into which tool. A base-layer skill every lawyer needs.
  • Tool judgment. Knowing which task suits a general assistant, which needs a vetted legal tool, and which should not touch AI at all.
Questions to ask your team If we could only teach one skill, is it clearly verification discipline in how we actually train? Which of the four is our weakest today, and who owns closing it?
Self-studyReskill vs hire - and how to build each skill3 min read▶

Most legal-AI readiness is built by reskilling the lawyers you already have; the domain judgment they bring is the hard part, and the AI layer sits on top of it. Hiring is for the champion role and specialist legal-ops capacity, not for replacing lawyers.

SkillHow to build it
Verification disciplinePractice with real fabricated-citation examples; make verification-before-filing a written, owned rule.
Prompt literacyShort hands-on sessions on real (non-confidential) tasks; share a library of prompts that worked.
Confidentiality awarenessA clear, memorable line - what goes in which tool - reinforced until it is reflex, not a lookup.
Tool judgmentCase-based practice matching task to tool; let the champion curate which tool for which job.

Notice that every row is teaching, not recruiting. The champion you may hire; the verification culture you build in the lawyers you have.

Self-studySignals your readiness effort is working2 min read▶

Adoption numbers flatter you; readiness is subtler to see. Watch for the behaviours that show skill is landing, not just usage.

  • Lawyers catch the tool. People routinely spot and correct AI errors rather than passing them through. Caught mistakes are a good sign, not a bad one.
  • Confidentiality is reflex. Nobody has to think twice about what goes into which tool - the line is internalised.
  • The champion is busy the right way. They are vetting tools and sharing prompts, not firefighting near-misses.
  • Questions get better. The team asks "should this even touch AI?" instead of "how do I make it use AI?"

If adoption is high but these signals are absent, you have usage without readiness - the exact gap this session exists to close.

Part 3 · covers legal-tech vetting + the three doors

Choosing legal tech - buy vs build vs blend 7 min live

With the team building, the tool question becomes tractable. There are three doors - buy a specialized platform, build in-house, or blend the two - and one discipline that applies to all of them: vet hard before you commit, because a poorly-grounded tool imports the exact risk your team is learning to avoid.

LiveThe three doors, and how to vet a vendor4 min▶

Most legal teams should buy specialized and blend selectively; few should build from scratch. Whichever door, the vetting is the same.

DoorWhen it fits
Buy specializedThe default - legal-trained tools grounded in real databases, with confidentiality terms built for client work.
Build in-houseRare - only with real engineering capacity and a workflow no vendor serves; you own the grounding and the risk.
BlendCommon in practice - vetted platforms for client work, plus general assistants for non-confidential drafting.

Vet every legal-AI vendor against four things before you sign:

  • Grounding + hallucination rate. Does it cite to real, verifiable sources, and what is its measured error rate on legal tasks? A tool that cannot show its sources cannot be verified.
  • Confidentiality + data-training terms. What happens to the data you send? Do they train on your inputs, and can you switch that off? Get it in writing.
  • Security. Encryption, access controls, and where your data is stored and processed - the basics a client would expect you to have checked.
  • Auditability. Can you see and reconstruct what the tool did on a given matter? Without a trail you cannot supervise it or defend it.
Questions to ask your team Which of the three doors are we actually walking through, and why? Have we vetted our current tools against all four criteria, or did we buy on a demo? Who owns re-vetting when the tool changes?
Self-studyThe vendor-vetting checklist2 min read▶

A short list to run before any legal-AI tool touches a live matter:

  • Grounding: cites verifiable sources; a stated approach to reducing hallucination; no tool claims zero.
  • Confidentiality: written terms protecting your data; no training on your inputs, or an off switch.
  • Security: encryption in transit and at rest; access controls; clear data location and retention.
  • Auditability: a usable trail of what the tool did, so a lawyer can supervise and defend the work.
  • Fit: serves a real workflow your team has, not a capability you will never use.

This is the checklist you complete in the exercise. Keep it - it works on the next tool too.

Leader exercise

Skills self-assessment + a vendor-vetting checklist ◐ 20-30 min with your team

Two artifacts, both usable this week: an honest read on where your team's skills stand, and a checklist you run on your next tool decision. Do this with whoever owns training and legal-tech procurement.

  • Map the ladder. Place your team on the four rungs - shared literacy, AI-literate lawyers, a champion, leaders setting the tone. Which rung is thinnest?
  • Score the four skills. Rate your team on verification discipline, prompt literacy, confidentiality awareness, and tool judgment. Name the owner for the weakest.
  • Reskill or hire. For each gap, decide: teach existing lawyers, or hire (usually only for the champion role). Most rows should be teach.
  • Pick a real tool. Take one tool you use or are considering and run it through the four vetting criteria - grounding, confidentiality, security, auditability. Note what you cannot answer.
  • Write it down. Turn the two into a one-page skills-map and a reusable vendor-vetting checklist, and share them.
Questions to ask your team What is our single biggest readiness gap - a missing skill, a missing champion, or an unvetted tool? What is the smallest next step that closes it, and who owns it by when?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Of the skills your lawyers need to use AI safely, which one most protects the license?

Verification discipline is the #1 skill. Prompting makes the tool useful, but verification is what keeps a lawyer out of the fabricated-citation sanctions story. Teach it first and hardest.

2 · Only about 35% of your lawyers feel AI-ready. What closes the gap fastest?

Readiness is a people problem. A mandate without skill manufactures risk - it produces usage and liability at once. Training, culture, and a champion build the judgment a mandate only assumes.

3 · A vendor pitches a legal-AI tool. What must you vet before it touches a live matter?

No tool is zero-risk. Vet grounding and error rate, what happens to your data and whether they train on it, security, and whether you can audit what it did. A poorly-grounded tool imports the exact risk your team is learning to avoid.
