Part 1 · covers Research.com AI-for-legal + the hallucination canon

What AI actually is - for a lawyer 6 min live

A large language model predicts the most likely next words. For a lawyer that makes it a fast, tireless junior associate for drafting, summarizing, and structuring - and a dangerous one, because it will confidently state law that does not exist. It is genuinely useful over documents you give it; it is a liability the moment you trust it for the law itself.

LiveThe fabricated-case trap - the failure that got lawyers sanctioned3 min▶

Because the model generates the most likely-sounding text, "what case supports this?" produces a case that sounds exactly right - correct-looking name, reporter, court, year - and does not exist. In 2023 a New York lawyer filed a brief with ChatGPT-generated citations to cases that were never decided, and was sanctioned. It has happened repeatedly since. Legal-specific tools reduce the risk; none eliminate it. Verification is not a best practice here - it is a professional duty.

  • Confidence is not authority. A fluent citation tells you nothing about whether the case is real or says what the AI claims.
  • It fabricates holdings too. Even a real case can be miscited for a proposition it never stood for. Read the actual opinion.
  • The duty is on you. Competence (Model Rule 1.1) and candor to the tribunal (3.3) mean you own every authority you file - "the AI generated it" is no defence.
★ Try it now (any general chat AI) - watch it inventGive me three leading cases, with full citations, holding that a party can rescind a contract for a unilateral mistake. Be specific and confident.
Real world

The brief that cited cases that never existed. The filing looked immaculate - proper Bluebook citations, plausible holdings, confident prose. Opposing counsel could not find a single case, because none were real. Sanctions, a public order, and a career dent followed. The tool did not fail quietly; it failed confidently, which is worse. That is the exact behaviour the rest of this course is built to defend against.

Part 2 · covers the 2026 legal-AI tool landscape

The legal AI tool landscape, 2026 5 min live

The right first question is not "which tool" but "is this safe for privileged, confidential client information - and does it ground its answers in real law?" There are two layers, separated by a hard confidentiality line.

LiveGeneral vs legal-specific - the setting that matters most3 min▶

The capability gap matters less than the confidentiality gap and the grounding gap.

  • General assistants (ChatGPT, Claude) - fine for drafting and rewording over non-confidential text you supply. Never for privileged or client-confidential material, and never as a source of law.
  • Legal-specific platforms (CoCounsel, Harvey, Lexis+AI, Spellbook) - trained on legal data, grounded in real databases, and offered under confidentiality terms suited to client work. They cut hallucination substantially - but not to zero.
  • Verification is universal. On any tool, a cited case is a lead to read, not a fact to file. The tool's marketing does not lift the duty off you.
One-question test before you paste "Is this privileged or client-confidential?" Yes - a vetted legal tool with proper confidentiality terms only. No, general public text - a general assistant is fine for drafting. Either way, verify the law yourself. That is rail two, and it never relaxes.
Self-studyThe 2026 landscape at a glance2 min read▶
  • Research + drafting: Lexis+AI, Westlaw AI, CoCounsel, Harvey - grounded in legal databases with citations you still confirm.
  • Contracts: Spellbook, and in-house tools like GC AI - drafting and review against a playbook (sessions b3-b4).
  • General assistants: ChatGPT, Claude - useful for non-confidential drafting and plain-language rewrites only.
  • The market signal: about 80% of firms are investing in AI, but only ~35% of lawyers feel AI-ready. The skill this course builds - using AI without abdicating the duty - is exactly the gap.
Part 3 · the duties every later session assumes

The rails: verify and own it, and guard the privilege 4 min live

Everything else in this course is technique. These rails are professional duties, not preferences, and they are what separate a lawyer who gets leverage from AI from one who becomes the next cautionary opinion.

LiveRail 1 - AI assists, the lawyer verifies and owns it2 min▶

AI drafts, summarizes, and suggests. You verify every citation and fact against primary law, exercise the legal judgment, and own everything that leaves your desk or is filed with a court. Competence now includes competence with the technology; candor means never presenting an unverified AI output as authority.

Real world

Verification is the billable skill now. The value a lawyer adds is no longer the first draft - AI produces that in seconds. It is the judgment to know what is wrong, the diligence to check every cite, and the ownership to stand behind the result. Treat AI as a brilliant, confident, occasionally lying junior associate, and supervise it exactly that closely.

LiveRail 2 - guard confidentiality, privilege, and the UPL line2 min▶

Never put privileged or client-confidential information into a consumer AI tool. Use vetted legal tools with confidentiality terms for client work, and remember that AI is not a lawyer - it does not give legal advice, and it cannot substitute for professional judgment.

  • Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6) applies to what you paste. A consumer tool may retain and train on it - a potential breach before you have even used the output.
  • Privilege can be waived by careless disclosure. The tool you choose is a privilege decision, not just a convenience one.
  • Unauthorized practice of law - AI output is not legal advice, and letting it stand in for a lawyer's judgment (yours or, worse, a non-lawyer's) crosses a line.
Say it out loud once "AI assists, I verify, I own it, and I guard the privilege." That sentence is the spine of the whole practitioner track.
Demo 1 of 2

The fake-case test - see it invent law, then catch it ★ 12 min · everyone builds

The single most important habit in the course, in one experiment. Ask a general AI for supporting case law, then verify - and watch confident, immaculate citations turn out to be fiction.

Open any general chat AI. Ask for leading cases with full citations on a specific, slightly unusual proposition (nothing confidential).

Note how authoritative the output looks - real reporters, plausible courts, confident holdings.

Now verify each citation in a real database or official source. Check that the case exists, and that it actually says what was claimed.

Count how many were fabricated or miscited. For most unusual propositions, it will be some or all. That is the trap, felt first-hand.

Repeat the ask in a legal-specific tool if you have one. Fewer fabrications - but verify anyway. The duty does not change with the tool.

The output to distrust (confident fiction)The leading authority is Caldwell v. Brighton Mutual, 219 F.3d 883 (7th Cir. 2004), holding that... [full, plausible, and quite possibly invented citation]
★ The habit to build - verify before you relyTreat every AI-supplied citation as a lead, not a fact: 1) Does the case exist in a real reporter or database? 2) Does the opinion actually hold what the AI claims, for this jurisdiction? 3) Is it still good law (not overruled or distinguished away)? Only what survives all three may be relied on or filed. You own that check.
Why this is demo one Every later session rests on this reflex: AI output is a draft to verify, never authority to file. Research (b2), memos (b6), and briefs live or die on it. Feel the trap once tonight so you never fall into it on a real matter.
Demo 2 of 2

Write your AI verification protocol ★ 10 min · build your own

A reusable artifact you will actually keep: a short "how we use AI safely" protocol. You draft it with AI, then edit it - which is rail one in miniature.

Give the AI the two rails and your context: your practice area, the tools you have (or have not) vetted, and the data you handle.

Ask for a one-page protocol with three lists: Green light (safe uses - drafting and summarizing over supplied, non-confidential text), Red light (never: privileged data in consumer tools, filing an unverified citation, AI as legal advice), and Always (verify every cite against primary law, use vetted tools for client data, a lawyer owns the output).

Edit it hard. Add your jurisdiction's specifics and your firm's approved tools. The AI draft is 70%; your professional judgment is the 30% that matters.

Circulate it. You will refine it in the leader session a3 (ethics) and the practitioner finale b10 (safe practice).

★ The verification-protocol promptDraft a one-page "AI verification protocol" for a legal team. Context: practice area [X]; vetted tools are [list, or "none yet"]; we handle privileged and client-confidential data. Build it around two rails: 1) AI assists, the lawyer verifies every citation and fact against primary law and owns the output - competence + candor duties apply; never file an unverified citation. 2) Guard confidentiality + privilege: no client data in consumer tools; vetted legal tools only for client work; AI is not legal advice (UPL). Format: three short lists - Green light, Red light, Always. Plain language, one page. Add a line that this is internal guidance, and that our jurisdiction's rules control.
Real world

The protocol that prevented the headline. A firm rolled out AI with one rule at the top of the page: no AI citation gets filed until a human has pulled and read the actual case. It slowed nothing that mattered and stopped the one thing that could end a career. The rule was cheaper than the sanction it prevented.
