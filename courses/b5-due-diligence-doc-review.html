Part 0

Why diligence is the high-stakes use case

Due diligence is reading at scale: hundreds or thousands of contracts, board minutes, leases, and emails, hunting for the terms and risks that change a deal or a case. AI can extract, summarize, and categorize across that whole set in minutes, which is a genuine leap. But diligence data is the most sensitive material a matter holds - it is confidential, often privileged, and disclosed to you under strict terms. So the confidentiality frame comes first, before any tool touches a single file: diligence data goes only into vetted tools with proper terms, and the lawyer verifies every conclusion. This session ties back to b1's rails and forward to the safe-practice finale in b10.

Part 1 · covers Research.com document review at scale

AI over large document sets 6 min live

Point AI at a document set and it does the reading-at-scale work fast: it extracts key terms, summarizes each document, categorizes them by type or issue, and surfaces the items that need a human eye. What it does not do is decide. It hands you a fast, structured first pass; you verify the conclusions and own the diligence that goes to the client or the deal team.

LiveWhat AI is genuinely good at across a document set3 min▶

The value is speed and consistency across volume - reading every page with the same attention, which humans cannot sustain over a thousand documents. But speed multiplies both good and bad: a wrong categorization applied consistently across the set is a consistent error. That is why the lawyer verifies.

  • Extraction. Pull the same fields from every document - parties, dates, change-of-control, assignment, termination - into a structured list you can scan.
  • Summarization. A short summary per document so you triage what to read in full, instead of opening all thousand.
  • Categorization. Group by type, counterparty, or issue - useful for organizing the review, not for concluding it.
  • Issue-spotting. Surface the clauses and documents that look unusual or risky as leads to investigate, never as settled findings.
Real world

The change-of-control sweep. A deal team faced 600 supplier contracts and one question: which ones let the counterparty walk if the target is acquired? AI pulled the change-of-control language from every contract in minutes and flagged 40 candidates. A lawyer then read those 40 and confirmed 11 real triggers. The AI did the reading; the lawyer did the finding. Neither number could have been reached the other's way in time.

Self-studyWhy verification is not optional at scale2 min read▶

At document-set scale the risk is not one wrong answer - it is a systematic one. If the model misreads what "change of control" covers, it misreads it the same way across every contract, and a plausible summary hides the gap. The lawyer's job is to sample and verify against the source documents until the pass is trustworthy, then own the conclusions.

  • Verify against the source, not the summary. A summary that reads well can still miss or invert a term. Open the underlying document for anything that matters to the deal.
  • Sample deliberately. Check the flagged items, a slice of the unflagged, and the edge cases - not just what the AI surfaced.
  • The finding is yours. What goes into the diligence report is your conclusion, reached with AI's help, not the AI's output passed through.
Part 2 · covers extraction, summarization, and privilege flagging

Extraction and privilege review 5 min live

Two workflows sit at the centre of diligence. The first pulls key terms and summarizes each document. The second flags documents that may be privileged or sensitive so they get human eyes before they go anywhere. In both, AI assists and the human confirms - the pattern is the same, the confirmation is non-negotiable.

LiveThe task-and-check table - who does what3 min▶

For every AI task in diligence there is a matching human check. The AI accelerates; the human decides. Keep this split explicit so nothing the AI produced is ever treated as a conclusion on its own.

AI does the taskThe human check that owns it
Extract key terms (change-of-control, assignment, term dates)Confirm each extracted term against the source clause
Summarize each document in a few linesRead the underlying document for anything deal-relevant
Categorize documents by type or issueSpot-check the buckets; recategorize the misfiled
Flag documents as possibly privileged or sensitiveConfirm privilege on each flagged item; never auto-release
Surface unusual or risky clauses as leadsInvestigate the lead; decide if it is a real finding

The left column is tonight's two demos. The right column is why a lawyer is still in the room.

Self-studyPrivilege flagging - a filter, never a decision2 min read▶

AI can help find the documents that are likely privileged or sensitive - anything to or from counsel, marked confidential, or discussing legal strategy - and pull them out of the pile for review. That is a triage filter that saves hours. It is not a privilege determination. Privilege is a legal judgment about who was communicating, for what purpose, and whether it was waived - and only a lawyer makes it.

  • AI narrows, the lawyer decides. A flag means "look at this", not "this is privileged". Over-trusting the flag is how privileged material gets produced or waived.
  • False negatives are the danger. A privileged document the AI did not flag can slip through. Sampling the unflagged set is part of the job.
  • Document the call. For each flagged item, record the human decision - privileged, not privileged, or needs a second look. That log is your defence.
Part 3 · the rail that governs this entire session

Confidentiality is paramount 4 min live

Read this before you open a single document. Diligence and review data is among the most confidential material you will ever handle - it is disclosed to you under strict terms, it is often privileged, and a careless paste into the wrong tool is a breach you cannot take back. This rail is louder here than anywhere else in the track.

LiveVetted tools only - the hard line for diligence data2 min▶

Diligence data never goes into a consumer AI tool. Full stop. It goes only into tools your firm has vetted, with confidentiality terms that fit client and deal work, ideally where data is not retained or trained on. The convenience of a general chatbot is not worth the confidentiality breach, the privilege waiver, or the disclosed-under-NDA violation it can cause.

  • Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6) covers everything in the data room. A consumer tool may retain and train on what you paste - a breach before you have read the output.
  • Privilege can be waived by disclosure. Putting privileged documents into an unvetted tool can waive the very protection your client is relying on.
  • Disclosure terms bind you. Diligence data usually comes under an NDA or clean-team agreement. The tool you choose has to honour those terms too.
The one question before any document goes near a tool "Is this tool vetted for diligence-grade confidential data, with terms that fit?" If you cannot answer yes with certainty, the document does not go in. When in doubt, it stays out.
LiveOver-trust is the other failure - verify before you rely2 min▶

The second danger is quieter: trusting the AI's categorization because it looks organized and complete. A clean, well-formatted output invites you to skip the verification - and that is exactly when a miscategorized document or a missed privileged item slips through. The lawyer verifies before any conclusion is relied on, every time.

Real world

The tidy summary that was wrong. An associate accepted an AI summary that a lease had no assignment restriction - it read clean, so no one opened the lease. The restriction was in a rider the summary skipped. The deal closed on a wrong assumption and the problem surfaced later, expensively. The output looked finished, which is precisely why it was not checked. Tidy is not the same as verified.

Say it out loud once "Vetted tools only, and I verify before I rely." On diligence data, that is the whole discipline in one line.
Demo 1 of 2

Extract key terms and summarize a document set ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Run the core diligence pass on a small supplied set of synthetic documents - nothing confidential. AI extracts the key terms and summarizes each; you verify against the source. This is the reading-at-scale workflow in miniature.

Take the supplied synthetic document set (three to five short contracts, provided - not real client data). Confirm it is non-confidential before it goes anywhere near a tool.

Ask the AI to extract the same fields from every document into a table: parties, effective date, term, change-of-control, assignment, termination.

Ask for a two-line summary of each document alongside the extraction, so you can triage which to read in full.

Now verify. Open each source document and confirm the extracted terms match. Note anything the AI missed, inverted, or invented.

Correct the table by hand. The verified table - not the AI's first draft - is the diligence artifact. You own it.

★ The extraction + summary prompt (synthetic docs only)You are helping with a first-pass document review. For each document I paste below, produce one row in a table with these columns: Document, Parties, Effective date, Term, Change-of-control clause (quote it or "none found"), Assignment clause (quote or "none"), Termination triggers. Then add a two-line plain-language summary of each document. Rules: quote the exact clause text where a column asks for it, so I can verify against the source. If a field is not present, say "none found" - do not infer or guess. Flag any clause you are unsure about for my review. These are synthetic training documents.
The verify step is the point The extraction looks authoritative because it is a neat table - which is exactly why you open every source document and confirm. A miscopied date or a missed clause, applied across a set, is a real diligence error. The table becomes trustworthy only after you have checked it.
Demo 2 of 2

A privilege and sensitivity flagging pass ★ 8 min · build your own

Run the triage filter: AI flags documents that may be privileged or sensitive, and you confirm each one. The AI narrows the pile; the privilege call stays yours.

Using the same synthetic set (plus a couple of supplied documents seeded to look privileged - to/from "counsel", marked confidential), ask the AI to flag candidates for review.

Ask it to give a reason for each flag - who is communicating, why it looks privileged or sensitive - and to list what it did not flag, so you can sample that too.

Go through every flagged item and make the human call: privileged, not privileged, or needs a closer look. The flag is a lead, not a decision.

Sample the unflagged set. Confirm nothing privileged slipped through unflagged - false negatives are the real risk.

Log each decision. That record of who decided and why is your defence if a privilege question is ever raised.

★ The privilege-flagging prompt (you confirm each call)Review the synthetic documents below and flag any that may be privileged or sensitive - communications to or from counsel, documents marked confidential or legal, or anything discussing legal strategy. For each flag, give: the document, a one-line reason, and your confidence (low/medium/high). Then list the documents you did NOT flag, so I can sample them. Do not make a privilege determination - only surface candidates for my review. I will confirm each call myself. These are synthetic training documents.
Real world

The flag that saved a waiver. During a document production, an AI pass flagged an email chain the review team had queued to produce - it spotted a lawyer copied deep in the thread. A human confirmed it was privileged legal advice and pulled it. Produced, it would have waived privilege on the whole subject. The AI did not decide; it surfaced the candidate in time for a lawyer to decide. That is the pattern, exactly.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Run the extraction-and-summary pass on the full supplied synthetic set. Verify every extracted term against its source and count what the AI missed or inverted.
  • Run the privilege-flagging pass and confirm each flag yourself. Then sample the unflagged set - did anything privileged slip through?
  • Write your team's one-line diligence rule: which vetted tools are cleared for diligence-grade data, and which are never used. If the answer is "none cleared yet", raise it.
  • Draft your privilege-decision log format - document, decision, who decided, why - so it is ready before a real review.
  • Optional: on a past diligence matter, estimate how long the extraction pass would have saved, and where a human check would still have been essential. Bring it to b6.
