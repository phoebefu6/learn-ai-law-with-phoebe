Part 0

Why draft from a playbook

You already know AI drafts fast (b1) and that you verify everything (b1-b2). Contract drafting is where speed pays off most - and where a generic draft is most dangerous, because a plausible-looking clause can quietly give away a position you would never concede. The fix is not to draft less with AI; it is to draft from your own material. Feed the model your clause library and fallback positions, and it produces a first pass that already reflects your standards. Then you do the part only a lawyer can: edit, judge, and own each term. A reminder before we start: this is education for legal professionals, not legal advice, and professional-conduct rules vary by jurisdiction - verify against your bar.

Part 1 · covers Research.com drafting + Spellbook playbook drafting

Draft from your playbook, not the model's memory 6 min live

A model with no instructions drafts a clause that reads well and represents nobody in particular - a blend of every contract it has ever seen. That average is where overreaching or non-standard terms sneak in. Give it your standards - clause library, templates, fallback positions - and the draft starts from your firm's positions. The pipeline is simple, and the last step never moves: the lawyer owns the terms.

LiveGeneric draft vs playbook draft - the difference you can feel3 min▶

Ask a model to "draft a limitation-of-liability clause" and it returns something competent and unaligned - maybe a mutual cap, maybe a carve-out you never wanted, maybe a number pulled from nowhere. Now hand it your standard clause and your fallback, and ask it to draft to that. Same speed, completely different starting point: the output reflects your positions, and your edits are refinements, not rescues.

  • The average is not your position. Without your standards, the model drafts the internet's median clause - which represents neither side's interests, and quietly favors whoever the training data favored.
  • Your material is the anchor. Provide the clause you want as the model, plus the fallback you would accept. The draft lands between them, on your terms.
  • You own every term. The AI proposes; you decide. Read each clause as if you drafted it by hand - because for professional-responsibility purposes, you did.
★ Try it now (vetted tool, non-confidential) - anchor to your standardHere is our standard mutual limitation-of-liability clause: [paste standard]. Here is the fallback we will accept: [paste fallback]. Draft a limitation-of-liability clause for a SaaS services agreement using our standard as the model. Do not add carve-outs or terms not present in either version. Flag anything you changed.
Real world

The clause that gave away the cap. A team asked an assistant for a "standard" indemnity clause and pasted it in with light edits. It read fluently - and it uncapped their client's liability, because the model's average indemnity favored the counterparty. Nobody chose that; the draft chose it. Drafting from the firm's own capped clause would have started the draft on the right side of the line.

Self-studyWhat "your playbook" actually contains2 min read▶

A drafting playbook is not a mystery - it is the material you already rely on, gathered so the model can use it.

  • Standard clauses: your preferred wording for the terms you draft most - liability, indemnity, IP, confidentiality, termination.
  • Fallback positions: the version you will accept when you cannot get the standard, with the line you will not cross below it.
  • Templates: the shape of the agreement - defined terms, party roles, governing law, structure you reuse.
  • Notes on positions: which side you act for, and what "good" looks like for that party in each clause.

Feed these as examples and the model drafts inside your lines. This is the setup for Demo 2, where you turn the playbook into a reusable prompt.

Part 2 · covers few-shot drafting with clause libraries

Clause libraries + fallbacks 5 min live

The mechanism that makes playbook drafting work is few-shot: give the model examples of what good looks like, and it drafts in that shape. Supply your standard and fallback clauses as examples, name the governing law and defined terms, state which party you act for - and then watch the output, because the model still likes to add helpful-looking terms you did not ask for.

LiveFeed examples, set the frame, then watch for overreach3 min▶

Three moves make a draft yours, and one habit keeps it clean.

MoveWhat you give the model
Few-shot the clausesYour standard clause + fallback as worked examples - the model drafts in that voice and structure
Set governing lawName the jurisdiction so terms and enforceability match - not a generic US-flavored default
Fix defined terms + partiesGive the defined terms and which party you act for, so the draft favors the right side consistently
Watch for non-standard addsRead for terms the model invented - extra carve-outs, novel obligations, an "industry-standard" clause you never approved
The overreach check After every draft, ask one question: "Is there anything here I did not ask for?" The model adds plausible-sounding terms to be helpful - a carve-out, a broader indemnity, a longer term. Strike anything that is not in your playbook or that you did not choose. Added-but-unnoticed is how a draft drifts off your positions.
Self-studyBuilding a clause library you can reuse2 min read▶
  • Start with your top ten. The clauses you draft most often carry the most leverage - capture the standard and fallback for each.
  • Label the party. A liability clause "good for the customer" is different from one "good for the vendor" - keep both, labeled, so the model picks the right one.
  • Keep it current. When a negotiation moves your position, update the library. A stale playbook drafts stale terms.
  • Legal-specific tools help. Platforms like Spellbook can draft against a clause library and flag non-standard language - but the review and the ownership stay with you.
Part 3 · the guardrails every draft assumes

Grounding + guardrails 4 min live

Playbook drafting only holds if the model stays on your material and flags when it strays. Three guardrails do that work - and one of them is the confidentiality rail you have carried since b1.

LiveApproved language only, flag the invented, no confidential terms in consumer tools2 min▶

State these in the prompt and hold them in the review.

  • Use only your approved language. Tell the model to draft from the clauses you supplied and not to substitute its own "standard" wording. Grounding it in your material is what keeps the draft yours.
  • Flag anything invented or non-standard. Ask the model to mark any term it added or changed, so you can see what came from your playbook and what came from its memory. What is flagged, you can check; what is silent, you might miss.
  • No confidential terms in consumer tools. Draft with non-confidential or synthetic examples in general assistants; put real client terms only in vetted legal tools with confidentiality terms. Rail two, unchanged.
The silent overreach to catch...and to be comprehensive, we have added a broad mutual indemnification covering all third-party claims, and extended the survival period to five years, consistent with industry practice.
The one-line guardrail Add this to every drafting prompt: "Draft only from the clauses I supplied. Do not add terms, carve-outs, or obligations I did not include. Mark anything you changed or invented." It turns silent overreach into a flagged edit you control.
Demo 1 of 2

Draft from a playbook - and own each term ★ 13 min · everyone builds

The core skill of the session, done once so it becomes a habit. Take a supplied playbook, draft a clause or a short agreement from it, then edit until every term is one you would stand behind. Use only the non-confidential sample material provided - no client data.

Open a vetted tool. Paste the supplied standard clause and its fallback as your examples, plus the governing law, defined terms, and which party you act for.

Ask the model to draft the clause (or a short two-to-three-clause agreement) using your standard as the model and staying inside your material. Tell it to flag anything it changed.

Read the draft as if you wrote it. Check every term against the playbook: is this our position, or the model's average?

Strike any non-standard or overreaching term the model added. Edit the wording to your voice. This is where you exercise judgment and take ownership.

Do a final pass against the fallback: nothing has drifted below the line you would accept. Now it is your draft, not the model's.

★ The playbook-drafting promptDraft a [clause type] for a [contract type] governed by [jurisdiction]. Use these as your standards - draft in this voice and structure: - Our standard clause: [paste standard] - Our fallback (the least we will accept): [paste fallback] We act for the [party]. Defined terms: [list]. Rules: draft only from the language I supplied; do not add terms, carve-outs, or obligations I did not include; mark anything you changed or invented. Output the clause, then a short list of what you changed and why.
Why this is demo one Drafting from a playbook is the move that makes AI safe for contracts. Do it once here on sample material and the reflex carries into b4 (review), b6 (memos), and every real draft: give the model your standards, own every term, verify against your positions.
Demo 2 of 2

Build a reusable drafting prompt ★ 8 min · build your own

Do the setup once, reuse it forever. Package your clause library, fallbacks, and guardrails into a prompt template you can point at any new drafting task - so tomorrow's draft starts from your standards without you rebuilding the frame.

Gather three or four of your most-used clauses - standard plus fallback for each - as your starter library. Use non-confidential wording for the template itself.

Write the frame around them: governing law placeholder, party placeholder, defined-terms placeholder, and the guardrail line (approved language only, flag anything invented, no confidential terms in consumer tools).

Test it: point the prompt at a fresh clause request and check the draft lands on your positions with the overreach flagged. Tune the wording until it does.

Save it as your team drafting prompt. You will extend it in b4 with review and redlining, and refine the guardrails again in the finale b10.

★ The reusable drafting-prompt templateYou are drafting for our team. Draft only from our approved language below; do not substitute your own "standard" wording; mark anything you change or invent. Our clause library (standard + fallback): - [Clause 1]: standard [...] | fallback [...] - [Clause 2]: standard [...] | fallback [...] - [Clause 3]: standard [...] | fallback [...] For each task I will give you: clause type, contract type, governing law [__], party we act for [__], defined terms [__]. Guardrails: approved language only; no added carve-outs, obligations, or terms; no confidential client data in this tool - synthetic or non-confidential examples only. Output: the draft, then a change-log of anything you altered.
Real world

The prompt that paid for itself by Tuesday. An in-house team saved one drafting prompt carrying their five core clauses and guardrails. Every new agreement started from their positions instead of a blank model, and the overreach-flag caught two invented carve-outs in the first week. The setup took an afternoon; it saved a redline war on every deal after. The lawyer still owned every term - the prompt just started the draft in the right place.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Finish the playbook draft if you did not complete it live. Owning each term against a standard is the skill no summary replaces.
  • Build your starter clause library - three to five clauses you draft most, each with a standard and a fallback. Keep it non-confidential for now.
  • Draft the same clause twice: once with no instructions, once from your playbook. Compare how far the generic draft drifts from your positions.
  • Add the guardrail line to your drafting prompt and confirm it flags at least one non-standard term when the model tries to add one.
  • Confirm which vetted tool you are cleared to use for real client terms. Draft-along stays on sample material until that is settled.
