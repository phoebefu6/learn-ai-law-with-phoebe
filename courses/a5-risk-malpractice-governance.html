Risk, malpractice & governance: verification is the shield

Where does AI actually create malpractice exposure for your firm or department, and what governance keeps it contained? This session maps the real risk - unverified output filed as authority, confidentiality breaches, over-reliance, and inaccurate advice - then hands you a governance map and an AI policy outline you can put in front of partners this quarter. No code, ever. The verification duty is the primary shield, and it is yours to build in.

Part 0

Why governance is a leadership job

Your lawyers will use AI whether or not you have a policy - the survey data says roughly 80% of firms are already investing, while only about a third feel their people are ready. That gap is the exposure. The sanctions that made headlines did not come from bad technology; they came from ungoverned use - an unverified AI citation filed as authority. Governance is how a leader turns a fast, confident, occasionally-wrong tool into a supervised, defensible practice. Tonight you leave with the outline that does it.

Part 1 · covers the malpractice + hallucination canon

Where the malpractice risk actually is 12 min live

Four failure modes carry almost all of the exposure, and they stack. Unverified AI output filed as authority is the one that has already produced sanctions; confidentiality and privilege breaches through consumer tools are the quiet one; over-reliance and deskilling erode judgment over time; and plain inaccurate advice reaches a client. One control sits underneath all four - the verification duty - and it is the primary malpractice shield.

LiveThe four failure modes, in order of exposure4 min▶

Name them plainly so the whole leadership team is working from the same list. Each one has a control; together they are the governance stack above.

  • Unverified output filed as authority. The sanctions case: an AI-fabricated citation reaches a court because no human pulled and read the case. This is the headline risk, and it is entirely preventable.
  • Confidentiality and privilege breaches. Privileged or client-confidential material pasted into a consumer tool can be retained, trained on, and disclosed - a potential breach of the confidentiality duty and a privilege waiver before the output is even used.
  • Over-reliance and deskilling. When juniors let AI do the thinking, judgment erodes and errors stop being caught. The tool should augment competence, not quietly replace it.
  • Inaccurate advice to a client. A confident, wrong answer that is never verified reaches a client as guidance. That is malpractice regardless of which tool produced it.
Real world

The failure was governance, not software. In the sanctions cases the tool behaved exactly as designed - it produced fluent, confident, invented citations. What was missing was a rule requiring a human to verify before filing. The malpractice exposure lived in the gap between "the AI drafted it" and "a lawyer checked and owns it." Close that gap and the headline never happens.

Questions to ask your team Which of these four have we actually seen internally in the last year? Where would an unverified AI citation reach a filing today - and who would catch it? Do our juniors know that "the AI generated it" is not a defence?
LiveVerification as the primary shield3 min▶

Of every control you could invest in, one does the most work. If nothing filed or advised on is relied upon until a lawyer has independently verified it against primary law, three of the four failure modes are caught at the last line of defence. The competence duty now includes competence with the technology, and candor to the tribunal means never presenting an unverified AI output as authority. Verification is where those duties become an operating rule.

  • It is a duty, not a preference. Frame it that way with partners - verification is how the firm meets its professional obligations, not an optional quality step.
  • It is the new billable skill. The first draft is cheap now; the judgment to catch what is wrong and the ownership to stand behind it are the value.
  • Make it un-skippable. A verification checkpoint before anything is filed or sent turns a good intention into a control.
Questions to ask your team Is there a hard checkpoint today where a human verifies AI-touched work before it goes out? If not, where would it sit, and who owns it? How would we prove, after the fact, that verification happened?
Self-studyDeskilling - the slow risk leaders miss2 min read▶

The three sharp risks announce themselves. Deskilling does not. When a cohort of juniors grows up letting AI structure the analysis, the ability to spot a wrong holding or a missing issue can atrophy - and that ability is exactly what verification depends on. Governance has to protect the skill it relies on: keep humans reasoning, not just approving, and treat AI output as a draft to interrogate rather than an answer to accept.

Questions to ask your team How do we keep juniors building judgment while they use AI? Are we training people to verify, or just to prompt? What does our review process teach - checking, or rubber-stamping?
Part 2 · covers the governance + risk-control mapping

The governance map 10 min live

Governance is not a document you write once; it is a set of controls, each aimed at a specific risk. Pair every failure mode with the control that contains it, then decide who owns each one. The map below is the spine of the policy you draft in Part 3.

LiveModel, vendor, and insurance risk3 min▶

Two rows on the map are easy for a busy leadership team to skip, and both bite later.

  • Model and vendor risk. A tool's accuracy, its data-retention behaviour, and its confidentiality terms are all your exposure once your lawyers use it on client work. Approve tools deliberately; do not let usage outrun vetting.
  • Insurance and exposure. Talk to your malpractice carrier now, not after an incident. Ask how AI-assisted work is treated and what they expect your governance to look like. A carrier conversation is cheap; an uncovered claim is not.
Questions to ask your team Which tools are actually in use on client matters, and which have we formally vetted? Have we asked our carrier how AI-assisted work is covered? Who owns the approved-tools list, and how often is it reviewed?
Self-studyThe audit trail - proving the control worked2 min read▶

Governance that cannot be evidenced is hard to defend. A light audit trail - which tool touched a matter, and who verified the output before it went out - is what lets you show, after the fact, that the control operated. It does not need to be heavy; it needs to exist. The same record also tells you where AI is actually being used, which is the data your policy reviews should run on.

Questions to ask your team If a client or a court asked us to show that AI-touched work was verified, could we? What is the lightest record that would satisfy that - and do we capture it today?
Part 3 · mirrors the practitioner safe-practice session (b10)

The AI policy that holds up 12 min live

A policy that holds up is short, specific, and enforceable. It names the approved tools, states the verification rule as a duty, draws the confidentiality lines, sets disclosure expectations, requires training, and fixes a review cadence. This is the leader-side mirror of the practitioner safe-practice checklist your lawyers will follow - the two must say the same thing.

LiveThe six things the policy must state4 min▶

Keep it to one page people will actually read. Every element below maps to a risk on the governance map.

  • Approved tools. Name the tools cleared for client data, and say plainly that everything else is off-limits for confidential material.
  • The verification rule. No AI citation or factual claim is filed, sent, or relied on until a lawyer has independently verified it against primary law. State it as a duty.
  • Confidentiality lines. No privileged or client-confidential data in consumer tools, full stop.
  • Disclosure. When and whether AI use is disclosed to clients or courts, per your jurisdiction and any standing orders.
  • Training. Everyone using AI on legal work completes basic literacy and verification training.
  • Review cadence. The policy is reviewed on a set schedule because tools, rules, and case law move fast.
Questions to ask your team Which of these six do we already have in writing, and which are only assumed? Does our practitioner-facing guidance say the same thing as the leadership policy? Who signs off, and how does a new lawyer learn it on day one?
LiveWhat makes a policy actually hold3 min▶

Most AI policies fail the same two ways: they are aspirational instead of operational, and they never get reviewed. A policy holds when the verification rule has a real checkpoint behind it, when the confidentiality line is enforced by the approved-tools list rather than by hope, and when a named owner reviews it on a cadence. Write the version your busiest partner would still follow at 6pm on a filing deadline.

Real world

The one-line policy that prevented the headline. A firm put a single rule at the top of the page: no AI citation gets filed until a human has pulled and read the actual case. It slowed nothing that mattered and stopped the one thing that could end a career. Governance does not have to be heavy to be the thing that saves you - it has to be enforced.

Not legal advice A policy template is a starting point, not compliance. Your jurisdiction's rules of professional conduct, any court standing orders, and your carrier's requirements control - review the final policy with counsel who knows your bar.
Self-studyDisclosure - the line most policies leave blank2 min read▶

Whether to tell a client or a court that AI was used is the element most policies dodge, and it is a candor question, not a style one. Some courts now have standing orders on AI disclosure; some clients expect to be told; some matters are silent. A policy that holds up does not leave this blank - it points to your jurisdiction's rules and any applicable standing orders and says what your firm does by default. Getting it wrong in front of a tribunal is a candor problem, so decide it deliberately rather than case by case under deadline.

Questions to ask your team Do we know which courts we appear in have AI standing orders? What is our default on telling clients - and is it written down or just assumed? Who decides disclosure on a novel matter, and against which rule?
Leader exercise

Governance gap-scan & your top five policy lines ◐ 15 min · with your team

Two moves that leave you with something real: find the gaps, then write the opening lines of the policy that closes them.

  • Run the gap-scan. Take the six rows of the governance map. For each, mark whether the control is in place, partial, or missing, and name an owner. Circle every row that is partial or missing - that is your risk register for this quarter.
  • Draft the top five lines. Write the five sentences that would open your firm or department AI policy. Lead with the verification rule as a duty, then the confidentiality line, then approved tools, disclosure, and review cadence. Five sentences, plain language, enforceable.
  • Pressure-test. Read your five lines as your busiest partner on a deadline. Would they still follow them? If not, tighten until they would.
  • Assign. Give each open gap an owner and a date. A governance map with no owners is a memo, not a control.
Questions to ask your team Which gap, left open, would embarrass us most in front of a court or client? What is the single control we could stand up this month that removes the most exposure? Who has the authority to make the verification rule mandatory - and will they?

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Where does the largest, most preventable AI malpractice exposure sit?

The sanctions came from unverified, fabricated citations reaching a court - a governance failure, not a software one. It is entirely preventable with a verification control, and banning AI ignores the competence duty rather than meeting it.

2 · Which single control does the most to contain malpractice risk?

Verification is the primary shield - it catches three of the four failure modes at the last line of defence, and it turns the competence and candor duties into an operating rule. Tools reduce hallucination but never remove it.

A policy that holds up is short but specific: it names approved tools, states verification as a duty, draws the confidentiality lines, sets disclosure, requires training, and fixes a review cadence - each element mapped to a real risk.
