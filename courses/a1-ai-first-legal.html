Part 1 · covers Wolters Kluwer survey + Research.com AI-for-legal

The state of play 6 min live

AI is not evenly useful across legal work. It is genuinely strong at drafting, summarizing documents you supply, first-pass research when it is grounded in real databases, and structured review. It is dangerous the moment it states law or cites cases from its own memory - it invents them with total confidence. The story underneath is a skills gap: about 80% of firms are investing in AI, yet only about 35% of lawyers feel AI-ready. That gap is a leadership problem, not a tooling one.

LiveWhere AI genuinely wins - and the one place it does not3 min▶

The value is real and it is concentrated. AI earns its keep on work where a human already has the source material and is going to review the output - drafting, summarizing supplied documents, structuring, and first-pass review. It is dangerous exactly where it feels most magical: ask it for the law, and it produces confident, immaculate citations to cases that were never decided.

  • Strong: drafting and summarizing. First drafts of memos, clauses, and letters, and summaries of documents your lawyers supply. A human edits; the tool saves the blank-page time.
  • Strong with a gate: grounded research and review. First-pass research in a real legal database, issue-spotting, and structured redlines - useful, but every citation is a lead to verify, not a fact to file.
  • Dangerous: law and citations from memory. A general model will fabricate cases with total confidence. Legal-specific tools reduce this; none remove it. The value your lawyers add shifts to verification and judgment.
Real world

The output is cheap, the judgment is the value. A first draft that used to take an associate an afternoon now takes seconds - which sounds like the win, but the win is really elsewhere. The scarce, billable skill is now the diligence to catch what is wrong, the judgment to know what the draft missed, and the ownership to stand behind the filed result. Leaders who reward speed over verification are optimizing for the exact behaviour that gets firms sanctioned.

★ Questions to ask your team Where are our lawyers already using AI, and did anyone tell us? Which of our high-volume tasks are drafting-and-summarizing (safe to lean in) versus law-and-citations (verify-heavy)? Where would a fabricated citation do us the most damage?

Part 2 · covers the legal-AI maturity model

The three eras of legal-AI 6 min live

It helps to name where you are on the climb. Legal-AI moves through three eras: assistive (draft and summarize), augmented practice (grounded research, review, and diligence behind a human gate), and agentic workflows (multi-step, semi-autonomous, with a lawyer signing off). Most firms are honestly in era one or early era two. Knowing your rung keeps ambition and risk in proportion.

LiveWhat changes as you climb - and what never does3 min▶

Each era buys more leverage and asks for more governance. The mistake is skipping rungs - reaching for agentic workflows before your lawyers have the verification habit that era two demands.

  • Era 1 - assistive. AI drafts and summarizes over material a human supplies. Low autonomy, high review. This is where trust and the verification habit are built.
  • Era 2 - augmented practice. Grounded research, structured review, and diligence, always behind a human gate. More leverage, and the point where confidentiality and grounding choices really matter.
  • Era 3 - agentic workflows. Multi-step, semi-autonomous sequences a lawyer supervises and signs off. Real efficiency, real governance load - and not where you start.
Real world

The firm that skipped a rung. A team excited by agentic demos wired AI into a multi-step research-to-memo flow before anyone had built the habit of pulling and reading every cited case. The efficiency was real until a fabricated citation rode the whole way through to a draft that nearly went out. The fix was not better tooling; it was climbing back down to era two and making verification non-negotiable first. The human gate is not a phase you graduate from.

★ Questions to ask your team Honestly, which era are we in - not which era is on the vendor slide? What would earn us the right to climb one rung? Where are we tempted to skip a rung because a demo looked impressive?
Part 3 · the four jobs the rest of this track builds

The legal leader's real job 5 min live

Your lawyers will learn the tools. Your job is different, and only you can do it: set the ethics and verification bar, govern the risk and malpractice exposure, build the team and choose the tools, and sequence the strategy so trust is earned before autonomy is granted. The next five sessions each take one of these on. Under all of it sit two rails - state them plainly, and repeat them until they are reflex.

LiveThe four jobs - and the two rails under all of them3 min▶

Everything technical is delegable. These four are not - they are what leadership of an AI-first legal function actually means.

  • Set the bar (a3). Ethics, confidentiality, candor, and the verification standard. What "verified" means, and that no AI citation is filed unread.
  • Govern the risk (a5). Malpractice exposure, client-data handling, and model risk. Who is accountable when AI is in the loop.
  • Build the team and choose tools (a4). Roles, training to close the skills gap, and buy-versus-build on legal tech.
  • Sequence the strategy (a6). Which use-cases first, when to climb an era, and how to earn trust before granting autonomy.
The two rails - say them plainly Rail 1: AI assists, the licensed lawyer verifies every citation and fact against primary law and owns the result. Competence and candor duties apply - "the AI generated it" is no defence, and the 2023 fabricated-citation sanctions are why. Rail 2: guard confidentiality and privilege - never put client-confidential data in a consumer tool - and mind the UPL line: AI is not a lawyer and does not give legal advice.
Real world

The bar is set by what you tolerate, not what you announce. A policy memo saying "always verify" changes nothing if the first lawyer who files a fabricated cite faces no conversation, or if the partner who ships fastest gets the praise. Leaders set the real bar in the moments they choose to reward diligence over speed. The rest of this track gives you the mechanisms; this is the will.

★ Questions to ask your team Of the four jobs, which is currently no one's job here? Can every lawyer in the room state the two rails without looking? Where do our current incentives quietly reward speed over verification?
Self-studyWhy competence now includes technology2 min read▶

The duty of competence has always meant keeping up with the practice. The shift is that "the practice" now includes the technology lawyers use to do the work - and that reframes AI-readiness from a nice-to-have into part of professional obligation.

  • Competence includes tech. Guidance across jurisdictions increasingly treats understanding relevant technology, including AI, as part of competent representation.
  • That cuts both ways. A lawyer who refuses to understand AI and one who trusts it blindly can both fall short of the duty. The standard is informed, supervised use.
  • Leaders carry it too. Supervision duties mean you are accountable for how the lawyers and staff you oversee use these tools.
★ Questions to ask your team If a regulator asked how we ensure competent, supervised AI use, could we answer? Whose responsibility is it to keep the firm current as the tools and rules move? What is our plan for the lawyers who would rather ignore AI entirely?
Leader exercise

Map where AI helps vs where verification is critical ◐ 20-30 min · bring to a2

A short mapping exercise you will carry into a2, where it becomes a scored shortlist. The goal tonight is not precision - it is a shared, honest view of your own practice.

  • List the five to eight highest-volume tasks your function does - research, contract drafting, review, diligence, memos, client comms, and so on.
  • For each, mark whether AI's role is mostly drafting-and-summarizing (lean in) or mostly law-and-citations (verify-heavy). Be honest about the mix.
  • Flag any task that touches privileged or client-confidential data - that is a rail-two constraint before it is a value question.
  • Name which era each task sits in today: assistive, augmented, or aspirational.
  • Bring the map to a2. We will score each item on value versus risk and pick your first bet together.
★ Questions to ask your team Which task, if AI got it wrong, would cost us the most - reputation, a client, or a sanction? Where are we already leaning in without having decided to? What is the one task we should protect from AI for now?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Your associates can now produce a first draft in seconds. Where is the value - and the duty - now?

The first draft is now cheap. The scarce, billable, and professionally required skill is the diligence to catch what is wrong, the judgment, and the ownership. Rewarding raw speed optimizes for the exact behaviour that gets firms sanctioned.

2 · Which era are most firms honestly in today?

Most firms are in era 1 (assistive) or early era 2 (augmented practice behind a human gate). The risk is reaching for agentic workflows before the verification habit that era 2 demands is built.

3 · A partner says AI-readiness is a personal choice, not a professional obligation. Is that right?

The duty of competence increasingly includes understanding relevant technology, including AI - and supervision duties make leaders accountable for how their lawyers and staff use it. Informed, supervised use is the standard, not opt-out.
