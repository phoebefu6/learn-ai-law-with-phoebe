Part 0

Why this session sits at the centre of the track

Every other leader session assumes this one. You can pick the right use cases (a2), build the right team (a4), and manage risk (a5) - but if the ethics floor is not set, none of it holds. The professional-responsibility duties were written before AI and they apply to AI without amendment: a fabricated citation is a candor problem whether a junior associate or a model produced it, and confidential client data pasted into a consumer tool is a confidentiality problem regardless of how helpful the tool felt. Your job tonight is to translate five long-standing duties into a short, enforceable set of expectations your lawyers can actually follow.

Part 1 · covers ABA Model Rules 1.1 / 1.6 / 3.3 / 5.3 + UPL

The professional-responsibility duties AI implicates 8 min live

Five duties do the heavy lifting. Read them as a stack: confidentiality controls at the base, then verification of AI outputs, candor to the tribunal, supervision of the people and tools producing the work, and disclosure at the top. Weak at the base and everything above it is exposed.

LiveCompetence, confidentiality, and candor - the three that bite first4 min▶

Three duties carry most of the weight, and they are the ones your lawyers touch every time they open an AI tool.

  • Competence (Model Rule 1.1) - now widely read to include competence with relevant technology. A lawyer who uses AI must understand enough about its limits to catch what it gets wrong. That means verification is not optional; it is part of being competent.
  • Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6) - applies to whatever your lawyers paste into a tool. A consumer product may retain and train on that input, which can be a breach before the output is ever used, and can waive privilege.
  • Candor to the tribunal (Model Rule 3.3) - the duty behind the fabricated-citation sanctions. Filing an authority you have not verified is a candor failure whether AI or a human produced it. "The AI generated it" is no defence.
Questions to ask your team Do our lawyers understand AI's limits well enough to satisfy the competence duty - or do they trust it? What client-confidential data has already passed through a consumer tool? Who signs off that a citation is real before anything is filed?
LiveSupervision and the UPL line - the two that govern how you deploy AI4 min▶

The remaining two duties are less about a single lawyer's keystroke and more about how the function deploys AI at all.

  • Supervision (Model Rule 5.3) - you are responsible for the work of non-lawyer assistants, and an AI tool sits squarely in that category. A lawyer must supervise AI output the way a partner supervises a junior associate's draft: reviewed, corrected, and owned before it goes anywhere.
  • Unauthorized practice of law (UPL) - AI is not a lawyer. It does not give legal advice, and letting an AI output stand in for a lawyer's judgment - or letting a non-lawyer use it to do lawyer work - crosses a line. AI assists; a licensed lawyer decides.
Real world

The duties did not change; the assistant did. Every one of these rules has an obvious analogue in supervising a talented, fast, occasionally-wrong junior. The mistake leaders make is treating AI as software rather than as an assistant whose work must be supervised. Frame it as the latter and your existing professional-responsibility instincts already tell you what to do.

Questions to ask your team Who is the named lawyer accountable for supervising AI output on each matter? Where could an AI output quietly become the answer a client relies on without a lawyer's judgment in between? Are non-lawyers using AI in ways that edge toward UPL?
Part 2 · covers the fabricated-citation canon

The sanctions story - and the duty-by-duty map 7 min live

The abstract duties became concrete in 2023, when a New York lawyer filed a brief citing cases that ChatGPT had invented - proper-looking citations to opinions that were never decided. Opposing counsel could not find them because none were real. Sanctions and a public order followed, and the pattern has repeated across jurisdictions since. The lesson is not "AI is dangerous"; it is "the verification duty is not optional".

LiveWhat the sanctions established - and what it did not3 min▶

The story is often told as a cautionary tale about a careless lawyer. The more useful reading for a leader is structural.

  • Confidence is not authority. The fabricated citations looked immaculate - correct reporters, plausible courts, confident holdings. Fluency told the lawyer nothing about whether the cases existed.
  • The duty rests on the lawyer, not the tool. The sanction was not for using AI. It was for filing unverified authority. Legal-specific tools reduce fabrication; none remove it, so the duty is identical whatever tool your team uses.
  • The pattern is ongoing. This was not a one-off. Similar sanctions have followed in multiple jurisdictions, which is why a firm-wide verification expectation matters more than a hope that lawyers will be careful.
Questions to ask your team If one of our lawyers filed a fabricated citation tomorrow, what control would have caught it - and do we actually have that control, or are we relying on individual diligence?

Self-studyWhat "verify" actually means before you require it2 min read▶

If you are going to set a verification expectation, be precise about what it demands - a vague "check the AI's work" invites the same fabricated citation you are trying to prevent. Verifying a cited authority is a three-part check, and all three must pass before it is relied on or filed.

  • It exists. The case, statute, or source is real and findable in a primary database, not just plausible-sounding.
  • It says what is claimed. The actual opinion holds the proposition the AI attributed to it - real cases get miscited too.
  • It is still good law. Not overruled, superseded, or distinguished away in the relevant jurisdiction.

Only what survives all three may be filed. This is the concrete standard your team applies matter by matter, and it is the working version of the candor duty.

Part 3 · disclosure, consent, and verification-before-filing

Disclosure and the ethics stack 7 min live

Once the base duties are in place, three practical questions remain: when do you tell clients you use AI, when do you tell a court, and what has to be true before anything gets filed. This mirrors the practitioner finale (b10), where the same stack shows up as a working checklist.

LiveDisclosure to clients and courts - when it matters4 min▶

Disclosure is context-dependent, and getting it wrong in either direction has a cost. Under-disclose and you risk a client or court relationship; over-promise and you set expectations you cannot keep.

  • To clients. Where AI tools touch client data, informed consent belongs in your engagement terms. Clients increasingly ask how their data is handled; a clear, honest answer is both an ethics posture and a trust advantage.
  • To courts. A growing number of courts have standing orders requiring disclosure or certification of AI use in filings. Know the rules of every court your team appears before - this is now a filing-hygiene question, not a theoretical one.
  • Verification before filing. Whatever the disclosure posture, nothing gets filed until a human has pulled and read the actual authority. That single rule is the cheapest insurance against the sanctions story repeating on your watch.
Real world

One line at the top of the page. The firms that have avoided the headline tend to share one habit: a written rule that no AI-supplied citation is filed until a lawyer has independently confirmed the case exists and says what is claimed. It slows nothing that matters and stops the one thing that ends careers. Make that rule explicit rather than assumed.

Questions to ask your team Do our engagement terms address AI use on client data with informed consent? Do we track the AI-disclosure rules of every court we appear before? Is verification-before-filing written down, or just hoped for?
Self-studyThe ethics stack as a standing expectation2 min read▶

Turn the stack into four sentences your lawyers can repeat without a manual:

  • Confidentiality first. Client-confidential or privileged data goes only into vetted tools with terms that protect it.
  • Verify, then rely. Every AI citation, quote, and holding is confirmed against primary law before it is used or filed.
  • A lawyer owns it. A named, licensed lawyer supervises the AI output and stands behind the result - AI assists, the lawyer decides.
  • Disclose where it matters. Clients consent where their data is involved; courts are told where their rules require it.

These four are the checklist you assemble in the exercise, and the same spine runs through the practitioner track's safe-practice session.

Leader exercise

Run an ethics + confidentiality gap-scan ◐ 20-30 min with your team

Before your lawyers use AI on a live matter, scan for the gaps that create ethics exposure. Work through this with whoever owns tools, training, and matter supervision - and bring your vendors into it too.

  • Confidentiality line. Which AI tools are actually cleared for privileged or client-confidential data - and can you name them? If the answer is "none yet", that is the finding, not a failure.
  • Verification control. Is verification-before-filing a written rule with a named owner per matter, or an assumption? Write it down if it is not.
  • Supervision. For each way your team uses AI, who is the accountable lawyer? Any use with no named owner is a gap.
  • Disclosure. Do your engagement terms address AI and client data? Do you track court AI-disclosure rules? Note the gaps.
  • Assemble the checklist. Turn the findings into a one-page AI ethics checklist mapped to the five duties, and circulate it.
Questions to ask your vendors What exactly are your confidentiality terms for the data we send you? Do you train models on our inputs, and can we turn that off? What grounding and verification workflow do you provide, and what is your hallucination rate on legal tasks? Get these in writing before client data touches the tool.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · One of your lawyers files a brief with an AI-supplied citation that turns out not to exist. Which duty is most directly breached?

Candor to the tribunal (Rule 3.3) means you never present an unverified authority as real. "The AI generated it" is no defence - the duty rests on the lawyer, and this is exactly what got lawyers sanctioned.

2 · A lawyer pastes a privileged client contract into a free consumer chatbot to summarize it. What is the core problem?

Confidentiality applies to what your lawyers paste. A consumer tool may retain and train on it, which can breach confidentiality and waive privilege before the output is even used. Client-confidential data belongs only in vetted tools with protective terms.

3 · A team lead asks whether AI can simply give clients legal advice to save lawyer time. Your answer?

AI is not a lawyer. Letting an AI output stand in for a lawyer's judgment crosses the unauthorized-practice-of-law line. AI assists; a licensed lawyer decides and owns the advice.
