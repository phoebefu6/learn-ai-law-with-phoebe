Part 1 · covers the safe-practice risk canon

The safe-practice risk stack 6 min live

Legal-AI risk is not one thing - it is a stack, and a workflow is only as safe as its weakest layer. Filing unverified output gets you sanctioned. Pasting privileged data into the wrong tool breaches confidentiality. Over-reliance deskills the team. Missing disclosure breaks candor. No audit trail leaves you unable to defend any of it. Every control tonight maps to one of these five.

LiveThe five risks - and the one that ended in sanctions3 min▶

Read this stack as the list of ways a lawyer gets hurt by AI, worst first. Each one is a live case, not a hypothetical.

  • Filing unverified output - the sanctions. The 2023 fabricated-citation cases and every one since started here: an AI output filed without a human pulling and reading the actual case. This is the loudest rail in the course.
  • Confidentiality / privilege breach via the wrong tool. Privileged or client-confidential data pasted into a consumer tool that may retain and train on it - a potential breach and privilege waiver before the output is even used.
  • Over-reliance and deskilling. A team that stops checking because AI is usually right loses the judgment to catch it when it is wrong - and it will be wrong.
  • Missing disclosure. Not disclosing AI use to a client or court where the rules or an order require it - a candor failure.
  • No audit trail. No record of what tool, what prompt, what was verified, who signed - so nothing is defensible after the fact.
Real world

The brief that cited cases that never existed. Immaculate Bluebook citations, plausible holdings, confident prose - and not one of the cases was real. Sanctions, a public order, and a career dent followed. The tool failed confidently, which is worse than failing loudly. Gate two - verify every cite before filing - exists to stop exactly this, and it never relaxes.

Self-studyWhy a stack, not a checklist item2 min read▶

A workflow can pass four gates and fail the fifth, and the failure is the whole risk. A perfectly verified brief filed from a tool that leaked the client's privileged facts is still a breach. A confidential, verified, disclosed output that no lawyer actually read and owned is still malpractice waiting to surface. That is why safe practice is a stack you run in full - every layer, every time - not a single box you tick. The controls in Part 2 map one-to-one onto these five layers.

Part 2 · covers the professional-responsibility controls

The controls 6 min live

Each risk in the stack has a control that stops it. Together they are the standing rules for AI on client matters: vetted tools with confidentiality terms, mandatory verification of every citation and fact, disclosure where required, supervision of every output, an audit trail, and a review cadence so the whole thing stays current as tools and rules move.

Confidentiality and verification are non-negotiable Two controls never flex, on any matter, for any deadline: no client-confidential or privileged data goes into an unvetted tool, and no AI citation or fact gets filed until a lawyer has verified it against the source. Everything else is judgment; these two are duties.
Self-studyThe controls in one paragraph each2 min read▶
  • Vetted tools + confidentiality terms. Client data goes only into tools cleared for it, under terms that bar retention and training on your data. This is the confidentiality duty (Model Rule 1.6) made operational.
  • Mandatory verification. Every citation, quote, holding, and fact from AI is checked against primary law before it is relied on or filed. Competence + candor (Rules 1.1, 3.3). No exceptions for deadlines.
  • Disclosure where required. Where a rule, court order, or client expectation requires it, disclose that AI was used. Candor to the tribunal governs.
  • Supervision. A responsible lawyer reviews and owns every AI output - the same duty as supervising a junior (Rule 5.3). "The AI generated it" is no defence.
  • Audit trail + review cadence. Record what tool, what prompt, what was verified, and who signed; revisit the standing rules on a schedule as tools and rules change.
Part 3 · who confirms, and what happens when something slips

The safe-practice checklist + sign-off 4 min live

A stack of controls needs a single accountable person and a plan for when a control fails - because one will. This is the practitioner version of the governance in leader sessions a3 and a5: the responsible lawyer confirms every gate, and a correction plan catches anything that slips before it reaches a client or a court.

LiveWho confirms, and the correction plan when something slips2 min▶

Governance without a named owner is a suggestion. Every matter that uses AI has a responsible lawyer who confirms the checklist and signs - not the tool, not a paralegal, not "the process". And because controls fail, you decide in advance what happens when one does.

  • The responsible lawyer confirms. One named lawyer runs the checklist - confidential tool, every cite verified, disclosed where required, output owned - and signs. Accountability does not diffuse across a team.
  • A correction plan for slips. If an unverified cite or a confidentiality slip is caught, you know the steps: pull it back, notify who needs to know, correct the record with the court or client, and log the fix. Decided calm, not in a panic.
  • Ties back to a3 and a5. The leader track sets the ethics and malpractice-governance frame; this checklist is how a practicing lawyer runs it on a live matter.
Say it out loud once "Confidential tool, every cite verified, disclosed where required, and I - a lawyer - own it and sign it." That sentence is the whole safe-practice checklist, and it is the spine of the entire practitioner track.
Self-studyNot legal advice - and the jurisdiction note1 min read▶

Everything in this course is education for legal professionals, not legal advice, and it does not create an attorney-client relationship. The rules of professional conduct - competence, confidentiality, candor, supervision, and disclosure - are jurisdiction-specific, and the sanctions record and tooling move fast. Verify against your bar's rules and your regulator before you rely on any of this on a real matter. The safe-practice discipline itself is universal; the exact rule text is not.

Demo 1 of 2

Run the safe-practice checklist against one real workflow ★ 13 min · everyone builds

Take one workflow you actually built earlier in the track - research (b2), review (b4), or a memo (b6) - and run it through all four gates. This is the finale's whole point: prove one of your own workflows is safe to trust on a client matter.

Pick one workflow you built earlier and write down each step where AI touched the work - what you pasted in, what it produced.

Gate 1 - confidential tool? Was any client-confidential or privileged data involved, and if so, was the tool vetted with proper confidentiality terms? If not, that workflow is not safe as-is.

Gate 2 - every cite verified? Was every citation, quote, and fact checked against primary law before use? Mark any that was not.

Gate 3 - disclosed? Did any rule, order, or client expectation require disclosing AI use - and did you? Gate 4 - lawyer owns it? Did a named responsible lawyer review and sign?

Log the result: tool, prompt, what was verified, who signed. Any gate you cannot answer "yes" is a fix before this workflow runs on a real matter.

The workflow to distrust (gates skipped)Fast research memo: pasted the client's confidential facts into a free consumer chatbot, used its three cited cases directly in the draft, sent it to the partner - no cite pulled, no disclosure, no sign-off logged.
★ The safe-practice checklist to runFor this workflow, confirm each gate before it touches a client matter: 1) Confidentiality - any client/privileged data used only a vetted tool with proper terms? 2) Verification - every citation, quote, and fact checked against primary law? 3) Disclosure - AI use disclosed to client/court wherever a rule or order requires it? 4) Sign-off - a named responsible lawyer reviewed and owns the output? 5) Audit trail - tool, prompt, verification, and signer all logged? Any "no" is a blocker to fix, not a risk to accept.
Why this is demo one This is the session, and the track, in one exercise. If you can run all five gates against a workflow you built and answer "yes" to each, you can use AI in legal practice safely. That reflex - never trust a workflow until it clears the stack - is what the whole course was for.
Demo 2 of 2

Write your confidentiality + verification + disclosure spec ★ 8 min · build your own

Turn the controls into your standing rules - the short document that says how AI may be used on client matters, so the decision is made once, calmly, not re-argued under every deadline. You draft it with AI and edit it hard, which is rail one in miniature.

Give AI the three anchors: confidentiality (vetted tools only for client data), verification (every cite and fact against primary law before filing), and disclosure (where a rule or order requires it).

Ask for a one-page spec with clear "must" statements, the named responsible role that signs off, and what to do when a control slips.

Edit it hard: add your jurisdiction's rules, your firm's vetted tools, and your matter types. The AI draft is the skeleton; your judgment is the substance.

Add the audit-trail line and a review cadence, then circulate. This spec is the standing rule the whole toolkit runs under.

★ The standing-rules spec promptDraft a one-page "AI on client matters" spec for a legal team. Build it on three non-negotiable controls: 1) Confidentiality - client/privileged data only in vetted tools with proper terms; never a consumer tool. 2) Verification - every citation, quote, and fact verified against primary law before it is relied on or filed; no exceptions for deadlines. 3) Disclosure - disclose AI use to the client or court wherever a rule or order requires it. Add: a named responsible lawyer signs every output; keep an audit trail (tool, prompt, verification, signer); review these rules on a set cadence; correction steps when a control slips. Note this is internal guidance and our jurisdiction's rules control - not legal advice.
Real world

The one rule at the top of the page. A firm rolled out AI across its practice with a single line above everything else: no AI citation gets filed until a human has pulled and read the actual case, and no client data goes into an unvetted tool. It slowed nothing that mattered and stopped the two things that end careers. The spec was cheaper than the sanction - and the breach - it prevented.
